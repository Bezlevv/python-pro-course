A behavioral interview is a conversation about your past experience. The logic is simple: past behavior is the best predictor of future behavior. You're not tested on Python knowledge; interviewers watch how you resolve conflicts, work in a team, handle deadlines, and admit mistakes.

## The STAR method

Any behavioral answer is built from four steps:

- **S — Situation**: context. Where and when it happened, with whom.
- **T — Task**: the task. What exactly needed to be achieved and what your responsibility was.
- **A — Action**: actions. What exactly you did, not the team. Concrete steps.
- **R — Result**: the result. What came out, the measurable outcome, what you took away.

Rough timing: situation and task — 20–30% of the answer, actions — 50%, result — 20–30%.

## Typical questions

- "Tell me about a failure and what you learned from it."
- "Was there a conflict with a colleague? What did you do?"
- "How did you make a difficult decision?"
- "Have you ever missed a deadline?"
- "Tell me about a time you took initiative."
- "How did you learn something new when there wasn't enough time?"

## Story map

The best way to prepare is to build a map: 6–10 stories from your life, each covering several topics. For example, a story about an imposed deadline fits "stress", "prioritization", and "communication". Write each story in STAR format into a separate file — then you won't have to improvise at the interview.

## How NOT to answer

- **Negativity about former colleagues and employers** — a red flag.
- **Overgeneralizations**: "I always do everything on time" — without a story, that's empty words.
- **Answers that are too long** — more than two minutes is hard to listen to.
- **Hiding mistakes**: honesty about a failure is valued more than a perfect picture.
- **Answers without numbers**: a result without a number is not a result.

## Questions for the employer

At the end of the interview you'll almost always get the floor. Good questions show interest and maturity: "What does a successful first month in this role look like?", "How does the team do code review?", "What's the main technical problem right now?". Avoid questions only about salary and vacation — you can ask the HR manager those later.

## Summary

Behavioral interviews are won by preparation: a STAR story map, honesty, and structure. Practice telling stories out loud — at home, to a friend, or in front of a mirror.

<!--TASK-->
### Task 1
Write a STAR-method story answering "Tell me about a major failure of yours." Length — 250–350 words. Use a real case from studies, work, or a project where something went wrong. Important: the story should end with what you learned from the failure and what you did differently next time. Make sure all four elements are present: situation, task, actions, result.

### Task 2
Here is a bad answer to "How do you handle conflicts?":

> "I'm generally a non-conflict person, I've never had conflicts. And if something happens, I just try to be friends with everyone and avoid problems."

Rewrite it as a STAR-method answer. Invent a plausible situation (for example, a disagreement about choosing a technology or about a deadline), describe the actions and the result. Make the answer specific, with a measurable outcome.

### Task 3
Build a map of six stories. For each, list: the topic (leadership, conflict, mistake, tight deadline, learning something new, initiative), a short story title, and one line for each STAR element. No need to write them out fully — just drafts you can expand later. This is your personal preparation base for interviews.

### Task 4
Write an answer to "What are your weaknesses?" using the formula: honest weakness + how you work on it + what it gave you. The weakness should be real but not fatal for the role (for example, difficulty with public speaking or a tendency to go into details). The answer — 80–120 words, without self-deprecation and without "perfect" dodges like "I work too hard".

### Task 5
Write five questions you will ask the employer at the end of the interview. They should be about the product, the team, processes, growth, and expectations for the first month. Exclude salary, vacation, and schedule questions — they fit the HR stage. Justify in one line what each question shows (for example: "a code-review question shows interest in code quality").

<!--HINT-->
### Hint 1
First jot down the facts: when it was, what happened, what you did, how it ended. Then arrange them into the STAR template. Don't blame others — talk about your own decisions. The result must include a lesson: "ever since, I always..." Read out loud and cut everything that doesn't serve the story.

### Hint 2
Find the two main mistakes in the bad answer: overgeneralization ("never had") and passivity ("try to avoid"). Replace them with specifics. The situation can be educational: for example, an argument about which framework to use. Actions — arguments, compromise, a joint decision. Result — what the project won.

### Hint 3
Use the template: `Topic: ... | Title: ... | S: ... | T: ... | A: ... | R: ...`. Invent stories from real experience: studies, an internship, a team project, a hobby. If you have no story for some topic, note it and think about what case you can recall or how to get such experience.

### Hint 4
The formula: "I have a weakness X. I noticed it when... To handle it, I... This resulted in...". Choose a weakness that doesn't hit the role's key requirements but is honest. For example, "I was shy about asking senior colleagues questions" and how you overcame it.

### Hint 5
Good questions fall into categories: product ("What is the product's biggest pain right now?"), team ("How is code review organized?"), growth ("What growth tracks exist?"), process ("What does onboarding look like?"), success ("What does a successful first month look like?"). Write one for each category and add an explanation of why that question matters to you.

<!--SOLUTION-->
### Solution 1
An example STAR story:

> S: In my second year I worked on a team project — a Telegram bot for schedules. I was responsible for the module that parsed the university website's schedule. T: A week before the demo, my module broke: the site changed its layout, and the parser stopped extracting data. I needed to fix it before the demo. A: I panicked for the first half a day, but then pulled myself together: I studied the new layout, rewrote the selectors, added handling for the case when data isn't found, and wrote logs to a file. I also wrote a simple test that checked the parser returned a schedule for at least one day. R: By the demo the parser worked again, and the project was defended with an excellent grade. The main lesson: I realized external systems change, and now I always add tests and error handling to code that depends on third-party data.

What's going on here? The story follows STAR strictly: the situation sets the context, the task the goal, the actions show personal contribution and concrete steps, and the result contains both a measurable outcome ("demo", "excellent") and a takeaway. The key is honesty: the story begins with a failure (the parser broke), and the emphasis on what you did afterward shows maturity. At an interview, such a story sounds far more convincing than "I never had failures".

### Solution 2
An example of a good answer:

> S: In a team project, a colleague and I couldn't agree on which framework to use for the backend: he proposed Django, I proposed FastAPI because we knew it better. T: We needed to make a decision without delaying the start of development. A: I suggested comparing both options on three criteria: development speed, performance, and our experience. We set aside an hour, wrote a small prototype on both frameworks, and measured response speed. FastAPI won on speed, but Django won on team experience. We honestly discussed the numbers and chose Django because code quality matters more than a couple of milliseconds. R: The decision took one day, the project started on time, and a month later I learned FastAPI myself on a personal project.

What's going on here? The answer follows STAR: a concrete situation, a clear task, real actions with numbers and a result. Instead of "I avoid conflicts" — a story about how you resolve disagreements constructively: arguments, criteria, measurement. This is exactly what the interviewer wants to hear: not "I don't argue", but "I know how to argue about substance and find a solution".

### Solution 3
An example story map:

```
1. Leadership | "Took the team lead role at a hackathon" | S: team of 4 people, 48 hours | T: distribute tasks and get to the demo | A: made a plan, daily standups, integrated the code | R: took 2nd place, all tasks delivered.
2. Conflict | "The framework debate" | S: team project | T: choose the backend stack | A: comparison by criteria, measurements | R: decision in a day, project started.
3. Mistake | "Production went down after deploy" | S: educational service | T: figure it out and restore | A: rolled back the version, added tests | R: service back in an hour, no more errors.
4. Deadline | "Parser broke before the demo" | S: project a week before the deadline | T: fix it | A: examined the layout, added logs | R: defended with an excellent grade.
5. Learning | "Mastered Docker in a month" | S: needed to deploy a project | T: get containers running | A: course, exercises, personal practice | R: deployed the project, helped a colleague.
6. Initiative | "Automated reports" | S: manual routine in the team | T: save time | A: wrote a script | R: saved 8 hours a week.
```

What's going on here? Each line is a ready draft: topic, title, and four STAR elements. The night before an interview, it's enough to refresh the map in memory — and you'll quickly pick a suitable story for any question. If some topic is missing, honestly note it and think about what experience you can get right now: speak at a meetup, take leadership in a project, write an article.

### Solution 4
An example answer:

> Honest weakness: for a long time I was shy about asking senior colleagues questions — I was afraid of looking incompetent. I noticed it when I spent half a day on tasks that could be solved in an hour by asking for advice. To handle it, I made a rule: if I can't make progress in 30 minutes, I write a question to a colleague with a description of what I've already tried. This gave a double result: tasks started getting solved faster, and colleagues began treating me as someone who respects others' time. Now I welcome questions in the team myself.

What's going on here? The answer is honest: the weakness is real and recognizable but not fatal. The main thing is that there's work on it and a result. Candidates' mistake is to name a strength as a "weakness" ("I work too hard") or shrug it off ("I have no weaknesses"). Interviewers value self-awareness: you know your weakness, take action, and can show it.

### Solution 5
Example questions:

1. "What is the biggest technical pain of the product right now?" — shows interest in real tasks and readiness to dive in.
2. "How is code review organized in the team?" — shows you value code quality and teamwork.
3. "What growth tracks exist for a developer?" — shows long-term interest and ambition.
4. "What does onboarding look like in the first month?" — shows a serious approach to joining the team.
5. "How do you evaluate a developer's success in the first quarter?" — shows the desire to deliver value and align expectations.

What's going on here? All the questions are about the essence of the work, not the conditions. They serve two functions: they give you information for a decision and send the employer a signal about your maturity. The question about first-quarter expectations is one of the strongest: it shows you think about outcomes, not just salary. Avoid questions that are easy to answer "yes/no" — prefer open ones that expand the conversation.
